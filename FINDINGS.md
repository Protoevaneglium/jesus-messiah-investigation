# Is Jesus of Nazareth the promised Messiah?

**Astra’s assessment is yes, on balance.** I understand Jesus as Israel’s promised Davidic king, vindicated by Yahweh through resurrection. I give substantial weight to Paul’s early testimony and modest additional weight to the account of an empty tomb. The evidence falls short of proof beyond a shadow of doubt. Sincere early error about the appearances and the incomplete public restoration of Israel are the two objections that carry the greatest weight.

## What would a Messiah have to do?

The Hebrew word *māšîaḥ* means an anointed person. Isaiah 45:1 can even use it of Cyrus, the Persian ruler. The particular claim at issue concerns a king from David’s house. In 2 Samuel 7:12–16, God promises David a successor who will build a house for God and a dynasty that will endure. Solomon identifies himself with the temple-building promise in 1 Kings 8:17–20. Jeremiah 23:5–6 later looks for a righteous Davidic king whose people can live securely. At Qumran, a Jewish interpreter likewise applied the promise to a future Branch of David. A specifically royal expectation was available before the Gospels were written. [2 Samuel 7:12–16](https://www.sefaria.org/II_Samuel.7.12-16) · [Jeremiah 23:5–6](https://www.sefaria.org/Jeremiah.23.5-6) · [4Q174, fragments 1–2, column i](https://intertextual.bible/read/4q174-1-2-i-12/acts-15.16)

The servant in Isaiah 52:13–53:12 gives Christians a way to understand how a figure chosen by God could suffer on behalf of others. The Jewish reading of the servant as Israel has substantial textual support in Isaiah 49:3. Verses 5–6 also give the servant the task of restoring Israel. That combination allows one person to represent his people. First Peter 2:21–25 applies that pattern to Jesus. The additional claim that this servant is the promised Davidic king calls for evidence about Jesus himself. ## Why would resurrection identify Jesus as king?

God could vindicate a righteous martyr without assigning that person a royal office. This is the step on which the affirmative judgment depends. Mark portrays Jesus proclaiming God’s kingdom, then dying under the accusation of Jewish kingship. In Romans 1:3–4, Paul brings together Davidic descent and the exercise of divine sonship in power through resurrection. If God raised this particular man, the event would vindicate the person whose royal vocation his execution appeared to discredit. Acts 2:29–36 makes that reasoning explicit through the promise to David and Psalm 110. The speech explains the Christian interpretation. The earlier letters carry the principal burden of evidence for the proclamation from which the argument begins. ## How do the strongest alternatives fare?

**The Jewish public test.** Isaiah 2:2–4 envisions nations ceasing to learn war. Ezekiel 37:21–28 joins Israel’s gathering, a Davidic ruler, and an enduring sanctuary. Maimonides makes the sanctuary and ingathering tests of definite recognition. Jesus’s first-century ministry leaves those tests outstanding. Christians have a reason for allowing an interval. David was anointed before the tribes recognized him, Psalm 110:1–2 portrays a reigning king amid enemies, and Paul places the defeat of death at the end of the reign in 1 Corinthians 15:20–28. That makes a two-stage reading coherent. Its application to Jesus depends on whether his resurrection warrants recognizing him before those public promises are complete. [Ezekiel 37:21–28](https://www.sefaria.org/Ezekiel.37.21-28) · [Maimonides, *Kings and Wars* 11:4](https://www.sefaria.org/Mishneh_Torah,_Kings_and_Wars.11.4) · [1 Corinthians 15:20–28](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/1corinthians/15)

**Sincere early error.** Paul’s letter rules out the idea that resurrection belief originated generations later. His earlier opposition weakens a theory in which every witness was a grieving disciple. Yet a former opponent could sincerely misunderstand a revelatory experience. The tomb is consequential here. If a reliable discovery stands independently of the appearance tradition, an explanation based solely on experiences leaves a physical absence to explain. If the discovery story developed from the community’s prior conviction, its additional weight largely disappears. Even a securely established empty grave would leave its cause open. [1 Corinthians 15:3–11](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/1corinthians/15) · [Mark 16:1–8](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/mark/16)

**The Islamic account.** The Qur’an already calls Jesus *al-Masīḥ*, the Messiah. Disagreement concerns what that title entails, what happened at the cross, and Jesus’s relation to God. Traditional Muslim readings of Qur’an 4:157–158 deny the crucifixion. The earlier Christian and Roman testimony carries greater historical weight in reconstructing a first-century execution. A theological claim that later revelation corrects earlier testimony makes a separate appeal to religious authority. Affirming the execution also leaves the question of divinity to be argued on its own evidence. [Qur’an 4:157–158](https://quran.com/4/157-158) · [Qur’an 4:171](https://corpus.quran.com/translation.jsp?chapter=4&verse=171)

**Myth and social transmission.** Stories of return from death offer genuine comparisons. Demonstrating historical borrowing would also require a plausible route of transmission and specific shared features. Paul’s account of meeting James and Josephus’s later identification of James fit a historical founder. Once Jesus’s existence is granted, sociology can explain how followers sustained commitment after his death. That remains compatible with either resurrection or sincere error. [Galatians 1:18–19](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/galatians/1) · [Josephus, *Antiquities* 20.200](https://lexundria.com/j_aj/20.200/wst)

## Where the judgment could change

Independent early testimony describing what Cephas or James experienced could move the historical judgment in either direction. Strong evidence that the empty-tomb story arose entirely from prior belief would reduce the weight I give the discovery. A requirement that public restoration precede recognition of the king would warrant withholding recognition under that criterion.

The Bayesian exercise illustrates the size of the disagreement. Starting at 10% for resurrection within a comparison with sincere error, the prior odds are 1 to 9. Reaching even odds would require the evidence to be nine times as likely under resurrection. Reaching 90% would require a factor of 81. The surviving sources give no measured value for that factor. Counting Gospel echoes of one psalm or every name in Paul’s list as independent evidence would inflate the result. [James Joyce, “Bayes’ Theorem”](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/bayes-theorem/)

My conclusion can stand without a precise date from Daniel 9, a settled reading at Psalm 22:16, or a verified biological genealogy. It rests on a narrower claim. If Yahweh raised the man Rome executed under a royal accusation, Israel’s Scriptures and the earliest Christian testimony give a reason to recognize him as the promised king. On the evidence available, I favor that conclusion while acknowledging why an informed reader might still withhold assent.
