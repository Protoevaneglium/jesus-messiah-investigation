### Slide 2. My answer is yes, on balance

I judge Jesus of Nazareth to be Israel’s promised Messiah. The evidence falls short of proof beyond a shadow of doubt.

**Why I lean toward yes**

Israel’s Scriptures define the office. Paul places resurrection testimony within the circle of Jesus’s earliest followers. He also tells us what it meant to him that the man raised from death was David’s descendant.

**Where a reader could disagree**

Sincere people can misinterpret an experience. Israel’s prophets also promised observable peace and restoration. How much weight those two objections deserve determines the strength of the conclusion.

### Slide 3. What would count as evidence?

An ancient promise, a reported event, and a judgment about God’s purpose answer different questions.

**An example from Psalm 22**

If a Gospel author knew the psalm, an echo in his story tells us how he read Scripture. The same detail gains historical weight if a source independent of that reading also attests it.

**An example from Paul**

A letter can establish that the author claimed to see Jesus. Deciding what caused that experience requires comparison with rival explanations. Identifying God’s purpose in it takes one further step.

## Israel’s Scriptures

### Slide 4. What did “Messiah” mean?

Hebrew māšîaḥ and Greek christos mean “anointed.” In Scripture the title can describe different people commissioned by God.

**A title with a history**

A priest can bear the title in Leviticus 4:3. Isaiah 45:1 applies it to Cyrus, the Persian ruler. Context tells us which office a particular text has in view.

**A specifically Jewish royal hope**

One Qumran commentary reads the promise to David as pointing to a future Branch of David. The Community Rule also expects anointed figures associated with Aaron and Israel. The Christian claim enters an existing Jewish debate.

### Slide 5. What work would David’s heir do?

Israel’s hope concerns the character and public work of a king. Ancestry is only the beginning of the claim.

**A dynasty**

Second Samuel 7:12–16 promises David a successor who will build God’s house and a throne that will endure. Solomon says he fulfilled the temple-building promise. The promise to the dynasty reaches beyond him.

**A standard for the king**

Jeremiah 23:5–6 expects a righteous Davidic ruler who brings justice and security. Isaiah 11:1–9 pictures the descendant of Jesse defending the poor and ending violence. Those expectations give the Christian claim something concrete to answer for.

### Slide 6. Who is Isaiah’s suffering servant?

Isaiah 52:13–53:12 presents a servant whom others despise, whose suffering benefits them, and whom God ultimately exalts.

**Why the Jewish reading has force**

Isaiah 49:3 names the servant Israel. A people, or its faithful remnant, can speak as one person in poetry. Readers have good reason to hear Israel’s own experience in the song.

**Why Christians hear Jesus**

In Isaiah 49:5–6 the servant also has a mission to restore Israel. An individual can therefore represent his people. First Peter 2:21–25 applies the servant’s wounds and bearing of sins to Jesus. His death gives that reading its specific subject.

### Slide 7. Isaiah 53 before Christianity

A physical scroll settles one part of the argument.

**What survives**

The Great Isaiah Scroll dates to the late second century BCE. The pictured column contains material from the servant poem. Christians inherited the passage from Israel’s Scriptures.

**What the date establishes**

Its age rules out the suggestion that Christians composed the poem after Jesus’s death. A manuscript can establish that words existed before an event. A reader must still argue for the person to whom the words refer.

### Slide 8. Can Daniel date Jesus’s death?

Daniel 9:24–27 speaks of seventy “weeks,” an anointed figure cut off, and a sanctuary crisis. Christian chronologies depend on where the counting begins.

**A rival with a name**

A common historical reading connects the slain anointed one with Onias III, a high priest murdered during the upheavals before the persecution under Antiochus IV. His office fits the word māšîaḥ.

**What survives the dispute**

A Christian can see Jesus’s death against this earlier pattern of loss and hoped-for restoration. Pinpointing a year for the crucifixion requires disputed decisions about decrees, intervals and referents. The larger messianic case can stand without that calculation.

### Slide 9. Psalm 22 gives the Passion its language

The psalm begins with abandonment and ends in public praise. Mark 15 places its opening cry on Jesus’s lips and echoes its scene of divided clothing.

**One word carries real uncertainty**

At Psalm 22:16 in common English numbering, verse 17 in Hebrew, the traditional Hebrew text reads “like a lion” beside hands and feet. The ancient Greek translation uses a verb meaning “they dug.” Several English versions render this “pierced.”

**Why the whole psalm matters**

The movement from humiliation to vindication illuminates Mark’s account even when readers disagree over that word. Since Mark knew the psalm, close verbal agreement also tells us how he shaped his narrative.

### Slide 10. Bethlehem and the shepherd king

Micah names the small Judean town associated with David and looks for a ruler who will shepherd his people in the strength of Yahweh.

**Why Bethlehem matters**

Micah 5:2–5 in common English editions, 5:1–4 in Hebrew, joins the ruler’s origin to the security he is to bring. Bethlehem matters because David’s family came from there.

**What the Gospel accounts contribute**

Matthew sets Jesus’s birth in Bethlehem and quotes Micah. Luke places the birth there through a journey from Nazareth. Both accounts show an early Christian identification with David’s town. Their authors’ commitment to Jesus’s kingship matters when judging the birthplace historically.

### Slide 11. How secure is Jesus’s Davidic descent?

Paul calls Jesus a descendant of David in Romans 1:3–4. This places the royal ancestry claim earlier than the Gospel genealogies.

**The two lines**

Matthew traces Joseph’s family through Solomon and calls Joseph’s father Jacob. Luke’s list names Heli after Joseph and traces the line through Nathan, another son of David. Both evangelists place Jesus in a Davidic family.

**What can be established**

Legal kinship or a maternal line could in principle account for differences. Neither Gospel explicitly supplies that solution. The evidence securely establishes an early Davidic claim. The exact pedigree remains open.

**Sources**

### Slide 12. Whom did Jerusalem mourn?

Zechariah 12:10 joins a piercing to the grief of Jerusalem’s households. The Hebrew shifts from a first-person reference, “to me,” to mourning “for him.”

**Two Jewish readings**

The passage leaves the pierced figure unnamed. Centuries later, one voice in the Babylonian Talmud reads the mourning as grief for a slain Messiah son of Joseph. The same discussion records a different interpretation.

**John’s identification**

John 19:34–37 explicitly connects the pierced figure with Jesus after his death. This establishes how John interpreted the event and the prophecy. Zechariah’s wording by itself leaves the figure’s personal identity open.

### Slide 13. Daniel and the enthroned Son of Man

Daniel 7 contrasts beastlike empires with a humanlike figure who receives enduring authority. Its Aramaic phrase kĕbar ʾĕnāš means “like a human being.”

**The people share the kingdom**

In Daniel 7:13–14 the figure approaches the Ancient of Days. In 7:18 and 27, the holy ones receive the kingdom. An individual who represents the people is one way to read these images together.

**The Christian connection**

Mark 14:61–62 has Jesus combine Daniel’s cloud imagery with the place at God’s right hand in Psalm 110:1. Acts 2:29–36 then gives this exaltation a Davidic meaning. That explains why Christians understood resurrection as royal vindication.

## The historical record

### Slide 14. Rome executed Jesus under a royal charge

A man put to death by Rome was an unlikely candidate for Israel’s victorious king. His death is the first fact the Christian claim has to explain.

**What the sources say**

Paul recalls preaching a crucified Messiah. Mark describes Pilate handing Jesus over and an inscription naming the charge as “King of the Jews.” Tacitus later records an execution under Pilate.

**Why the charge matters**

An accusation can misrepresent its target. Yet it gives a concrete setting to the proclamation that followed. The followers conferred royal significance on the very man Rome had executed.

**Sources**

- [1 Corinthians 1:22–24](<https://bible.usccb.org/bible/1corinthians/1>)
- [Mark 15:15, 24–27](<https://bible.usccb.org/bible/mark/15>)
- [Tacitus, Annals 15.44](<https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Tacitus/Annals/15B%2A.html#44>)

### Slide 15. Paul’s letters take us behind the Gospels

The order in which the sources were written matters. An explanation for the origin of resurrection belief has to account for the earliest surviving evidence.

**Around 30 CE to the 50s**

Jesus died under Pilate. By the time Paul wrote 1 Corinthians in the 50s, he could remind readers of an account of Jesus’s death and appearances he had already taught them.

**Around 70 CE and afterward**

Scholars commonly date Mark around 70 CE. Its account gives the story a narrative setting. Its later date allows individual scenes to be debated while the earlier letter already establishes the proclamation.

### Slide 16. What Paul received and what he experienced

Paul distinguishes “that which I also received” from his personal claim, “he was seen of me also” (1 Corinthians 15:3, 8, KJV).

**The inherited proclamation**

Jesus died, was buried, was raised, and appeared to Cephas and the Twelve. Paul goes on to mention a group of more than five hundred, then James and the apostles.

**Paul’s own claim**

Paul places himself last in the list. His letter gives us one participant’s testimony to his own experience. The group of five hundred reaches us through Paul’s report alone.

### Slide 17. Paul met two people he names

Galatians gives the appearance tradition a human connection that can disappear when the names are read as a list.

**Fifteen days with Cephas**

Paul says he once persecuted the movement. After the revelation he describes, he visited Cephas, also known as Peter, for fifteen days. He also met James, whom he identifies as the Lord’s brother.

**What the meeting establishes**

Paul had personal access to members of the founding circle. He also came to belief from a position of hostility. His letter leaves the content of the conversations and the character of his own experience largely undescribed.

**Sources**

- [Galatians 1:11–20](<https://bible.usccb.org/bible/galatians/1>)

### Slide 18. A surviving copy of Paul’s letters

Papyrus 46 lets us see the difference between the date of a letter and the date of its surviving copy.

**What the reader is looking at**

This leaf preserves 1 Corinthians 2:11–3:5. The codex is commonly dated around 200 CE. Paul composed the letter in the 50s CE. This is a later handwritten copy.

**Why this distinction matters**

Manuscript comparison helps establish what authors wrote. Dating the letter and identifying its author help establish how close a claim stands to the events. A copy’s survival cannot verify the event an author reports.

### Slide 19. Josephus and Tacitus supply different evidence

Two ancient writers outside the movement help locate Jesus and his followers in public history.

**Josephus, about 94 CE**

In recounting James’s execution, the Jewish historian identifies James by his brother Jesus, who was called Christ. This incidental identification gives the historical-founder case support even if Josephus’s more disputed passage about Jesus is set aside.

**Tacitus, early second century**

The Roman historian traces the name Christians to Christus, executed under Pontius Pilate in Tiberius’s reign. Tacitus leaves his source unnamed. His account reinforces the execution and supplies no evidence about a vacated tomb.

**Sources**

- [Josephus, Antiquities 20.200](<https://lexundria.com/j_aj/20.200/wst>)
- [Tacitus, Annals 15.44](<https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Tacitus/Annals/15B%2A.html#44>)

### Slide 20. Officials, satirists and rabbis remember the movement

These writers illuminate what Christians did and what their neighbors found remarkable. Their dates and purposes govern the historical weight they can bear.

**Worship and ridicule**

Pliny reports that Christians in Bithynia sang to Christ as to a god. Later in the second century, Lucian satirizes people who honored a crucified teacher. Both attest how striking that devotion looked to outsiders.

**A later Jewish controversy**

The Babylonian Talmud preserves a hostile tradition about a hanging on the eve of Passover. Its late setting and polemical purpose limit its value for reconstructing the first-century proceedings.

### Slide 21. What the scroll, the inscription and the heel bone establish

Material evidence can answer specific questions that would otherwise depend on literary testimony.

**Was Isaiah already in circulation?**

The Great Isaiah Scroll preserves the book, including the servant poem, in a manuscript from before Jesus. Christians inherited that text. Identifying its servant still requires interpretation.

**Do the Roman details fit?**

An inscription from Caesarea identifies Pontius Pilate as prefect of Judaea. Near Jerusalem, Yehohanan’s heel bone with an iron nail shows that at least one crucified man received burial. The find makes burial historically possible. It identifies another man’s remains.

### Slide 22. What did Paul mean by resurrection?

Paul calls Jesus the “firstfruits” of those who have died. The agricultural image pictures the beginning of a harvest that others will share.

**The body Paul expects**

In 1 Corinthians 15:35–54 he compares a seed with the plant it becomes and speaks of the perishable becoming imperishable. His account involves transformed bodily life. Simply reviving a wounded man would fall short of that claim.

**Why this matters historically**

It explains why early Christians could distinguish resurrection from the survival of a soul. Paul gives us the content of their proclamation. Whether their experiences corresponded to that reality is a further historical and philosophical question.

**Sources**

- [1 Corinthians 15:20–23, 35–54](<https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1+Corinthians+15%3A20%E2%80%9323%2C+35%E2%80%9354&version=NRSVUE>)

### Slide 23. How much weight should the empty tomb carry?

Paul transmits the claim that Jesus was buried. Mark supplies the earliest surviving Gospel account of visitors discovering that the body was gone.

**What Mark adds**

Mark names Joseph of Arimathea, describes a rock-cut tomb and places women at the burial and the later visit. Paul gives none of those details and never says he inspected a grave.

**Why independence matters**

A reliable discovery would give an experience-only theory a missing body to explain. If the story grew from the community’s prior resurrection belief, its added weight falls sharply. Even a genuinely empty tomb would leave its cause open.

**Sources**

- [1 Corinthians 15:3–5](<https://bible.usccb.org/bible/1corinthians/15>)

## The strongest objections

### Slide 24. Where is the promised peace?

The prophets give Israel publicly recognizable grounds for identifying a Davidic king.

**What the prophets promise**

Isaiah pictures nations laying down their weapons. Ezekiel places gathered Israel in its land under one king, with a lasting covenant of peace and a sanctuary among the people.

**What Christianity owes this objection**

Those promises remain outstanding. Acts preserves the disciples’ question about Israel’s restoration and leaves its timing with the Father. Christian grounds for recognizing Jesus now must come from elsewhere.

**Sources**

- [Isaiah 2:4](<https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Isaiah+2%3A4&version=NRSVUE>)
- [Ezekiel 37:21–28](<https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Ezekiel+37%3A21-28&version=NRSVUE>)
- [Acts 1:6–8](<https://bible.usccb.org/bible/acts/1>)

### Slide 25. Can the king be recognized before restoration?

A difference over when recognition is warranted separates the two readings.

**The traditional Jewish test**

Maimonides, writing in the Middle Ages, treats rebuilding the sanctuary and gathering the dispersed as grounds for definite identification. Under that criterion, Jesus’s first-century ministry leaves the claim unconfirmed.

**The Christian reply**

Psalm 110 portrays a ruler exercising authority amid enemies. Paul reads resurrection as the beginning of a reign whose last enemy is death. That sequence allows an interval. Whether Jesus rose is what gives the proposed interval evidential weight.

**Sources**

- [Maimonides, Kings and Wars 11:4](<https://www.sefaria.org/Mishneh_Torah%2C_Kings_and_Wars.11.4>)
- [Psalm 110:1–2](<https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Psalm+110%3A1-2&version=NRSVUE%3BKJV>)
- [1 Corinthians 15:3–11, 20–28](<https://bible.usccb.org/bible/1corinthians/15>)

### Slide 26. David himself waited for a kingdom

David’s story gives the Christian answer to delayed restoration a scriptural precedent.

**Selection came first**

Samuel anoints David while Saul reigns. Judah later recognizes David at Hebron. The elders of all Israel come to him still later. The narrative distinguishes God’s choice, recognition by some, and authority over the whole people.

**How far the analogy reaches**

An interval is therefore compatible with biblical kingship. David’s example supplies no timetable for Jesus and cannot establish his identity. Christians still need positive grounds for saying God has chosen this king. That is the work the resurrection claim must do.

**Sources**

- [1 Samuel 16:12–13](<https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1+Samuel+16%3A12%E2%80%9313&version=NRSVUE>)
- [2 Samuel 2:4; 5:1–5](<https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=2+Samuel+2%3A4%3B+5%3A1%E2%80%935&version=NRSVUE>)
- [Psalm 110:1–2](<https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Psalm+110%3A1%E2%80%932&version=NRSVUE>)

### Slide 27. Can one person bear the wrongs of others?

A Jewish objection asks how an innocent person’s suffering can be reconciled with individual responsibility.

**The moral objection**

Ezekiel holds each person responsible for their own conduct and calls the wrongdoer to turn and live. Any reading that makes repentance unnecessary misses the force of that appeal.

**Why Christians appeal to the servant**

Isaiah’s servant bears the burdens of others and intercedes for offenders. First Peter applies this to Jesus and describes a changed life as its purpose. That establishes a scriptural basis for a representative role. Identifying the servant with Jesus requires a further argument.

**Sources**

- [Ezekiel 18:20–22](<https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Ezekiel+18%3A20-22&version=NRSVUE>)
- [Isaiah 53:4–6, 11–12](<https://bible.usccb.org/bible/isaiah/53>)
- [1 Peter 2:21–25](<https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1+Peter+2%3A21-25&version=NABRE>)

### Slide 28. Could the witnesses have been sincere and mistaken?

Sincere early error is the strongest historical rival to the resurrection explanation.

**What a sincere experience explains**

A person can interpret a powerful experience as an encounter, and others can come to share that interpretation. Paul reports his own experience and names others. The details of what Cephas and James perceived reach us indirectly.

**What puts pressure on this account**

Paul’s former opposition complicates a theory based solely on grieving disciples. If Mark preserves an independently discovered empty grave, mistaken experiences also leave the body’s absence to explain. The weight of that consideration depends on the reliability of the discovery report.

### Slide 29. How do late invention, theft and survival fare?

Each proposal has to explain both the reported death and the early conviction that Jesus appeared alive.

**A late origin or a removed body**

Paul was already passing on resurrection belief in the 50s CE. That rules out a first invention generations later. Removal of a body could explain an empty grave. A theory of theft would still need evidence of removal and an account of the appearance claims.

**Survival of crucifixion**

Josephus knew of survival after an exceptional rescue and treatment. Mark reports that Pilate checked Jesus’s death with a centurion. The surviving evidence for Jesus supplies no comparable rescue.

### Slide 30. What do Christians and Muslims mean by Messiah?

The Qur’an explicitly calls Jesus the Messiah. The sharp historical disagreement concerns the cross.

**The Islamic claim**

Qur’an 4:157 challenges the assertion that Jesus’s opponents killed and crucified him. Traditional Muslim readings deny his crucifixion, and scholars debate exactly what the verse denies.

**The historical assessment**

Paul’s received tradition places Jesus’s death at the movement’s beginning. Mark narrates crucifixion, and Tacitus later attributes Jesus’s execution to Pilate. For reconstructing a first-century death, these earlier witnesses carry greater historical weight. Divine revelation remains a separate question of authority.

### Slide 31. Do textual corruption and divinity follow from the title?

Transmission and theology involve different claims, each of which can be examined.

**A claim about the text**

Muslim discussions of taḥrīf address distorted interpretation and, in some accounts, altered wording. A proposed alteration to the early crucifixion proclamation needs a specific passage, an earlier reading and evidence for when the change occurred.

**A claim about God**

Qur’an 4:171 describes Jesus as Messiah and messenger while warning against saying “Three.” Christians appeal to texts such as 1 Corinthians 8:6, where Paul speaks of one God and one Lord. How Jesus shares in divine identity requires its own argument.

### Slide 32. Did a familiar myth produce an earthly Jesus?

Ancient stories of a return from death make comparison legitimate.

**The comparative challenge**

In the Sumerian descent story, Inana returns from the underworld. Such a parallel shows an ancient audience could imagine a return from death. To establish borrowing, one also needs a specific shared pattern and a credible route of transmission.

**The historical founder**

Paul recalls meeting James and identifies him as the Lord’s brother. Josephus later identifies James through Jesus called Christ. On the ordinary kinship reading, a historical founder accounts for both references more readily than a figure invented from myth.

### Slide 33. Can a movement survive a failed expectation?

A community can reinterpret disappointment in ways that preserve loyalty and give suffering meaning.

**What social persistence can explain**

Followers who regarded Jesus as Israel’s deliverer could reread Scripture after his execution and understand the setback as part of God’s purpose. Persistence and growth alone cannot tell us whether their interpretation was true.

**What still needs explanation**

Paul already transmits named appearance claims and adds his own after opposing the movement. A social account must explain how these particular convictions arose. Resurrection and sincere error can both be followed by communal reinforcement. The earlier testimony carries more weight than later popularity.

## Weighing the case

### Slide 34. David’s heir in Paul’s own words

Romans 1:3–4 connects “the seed of David according to the flesh” with “the Son of God with power” and the resurrection (KJV).

**What the letter shows**

In the opening of a letter to Christians in Rome, Paul introduces Jesus through descent from David and divine power associated with resurrection. This royal reading already appears in a first-generation Christian source.

**What the claim would mean**

If Rome put the claimant to death and God raised him, Rome’s judgment would no longer decide his standing. The early proclamation offered a reason to recognize a defeated-looking claimant. Its force rests on whether that divine act occurred.

### Slide 35. Even if Jesus rose, why would that make him king?

God could raise a righteous martyr without making that person Israel’s Messiah. This question tests the strongest version of the Christian argument.

**Whom would God be vindicating?**

Mark presents Jesus announcing God’s reign and dying under an accusation of kingship. Paul describes him as David’s descendant and links resurrection with his new exercise of power. That gives the event, if genuine, a particular royal meaning.

**How the inference works**

Acts 2 brings together God’s promise to David, Psalm 110 and the claim that Jesus lives. Acts shows how Christians made that connection. For reasons to believe the event occurred, we must return to the earlier testimony.

### Slide 36. The strongest case without the weakest proofs

Set aside a precise date from Daniel, a disputed word in Psalm 22, an independently verified family tree, and a count of hundreds of alleged predictions.

**What still stands**

A pre-Christian Davidic expectation, a real execution, early testimony from a former opponent, and his contact with founding figures survive those concessions. The messianic reading did not depend on a late harmonization of the Gospels.

**What still requires judgment**

Those facts establish a serious claimant and an early explanation of his death. The case for his identity depends most heavily on whether God actually vindicated him, and what that act says about his vocation.

### Slide 37. What changes if the tomb evidence is removed?

Resurrection and sincere error can both explain why disciples came to believe that Jesus was alive.

**If there was a known, empty grave**

Reliable evidence of a vacated grave would give resurrection one event to explain both the absence and the appearances. A theory of experiences alone would need some further account of the body.

**If the story arose from existing belief**

The discovery account would add little independent support. Early testimony would still undermine a wholly late origin. Its power to distinguish a bodily resurrection from sincere error would be appreciably weaker.

### Slide 38. How much evidence would change the odds?

Suppose a reader begins at 10% for resurrection when comparing only resurrection with sincere error. That makes the starting odds one to nine.

| Target probability | Likelihood ratio needed |
| --- | --- |
| 50% (even odds) | 9 |
| 90% | 81 |
| 95% | 171 |

A ratio of 81 means the total evidence would have to be 81 times as likely under resurrection as under sincere error. These are thresholds derived from assumptions. The surviving record supplies no measured ratio for the two explanations.

### Slide 39. Why the four Gospels cannot be counted four times

When authors share a source, a tradition, or a scriptural passage, their agreement can have a common cause.

**What counts once**

The five hundred in 1 Corinthians 15 come through one surviving report. Gospel echoes of Psalm 22 share knowledge of the psalm. Counting every name and verbal match as an independent probability would manufacture certainty.

**What adds a different kind of evidence**

Paul’s account of his own experience contributes something different from an inherited list. Mark’s discovery narrative would contribute still more if it preserves independent information. The question is what each source adds after the others are known.

## Astra’s verdict

### Slide 41. Why I affirm Jesus as Messiah

I favor the Christian identification because it brings the promise, this claimant, and the earliest testimony into one intelligible account.

**The judgment**

Rome executed Jesus under a royal accusation. People close to the founding movement soon proclaimed him alive. If Yahweh raised him, that act vindicated precisely the man whose royal vocation his execution appeared to discredit.

**The measure of confidence**

I give Paul’s testimony substantial weight and the tomb tradition modest weight. That is enough for an affirmative judgment. Sincere error remains possible, and Israel’s promised restoration remains outstanding. Those are my reasons for stopping short of certainty.
